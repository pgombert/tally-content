# The Month That Breaks Your Budget

I can tell you the exact moment most budgets die.

It's not a spending spree. It's the month when the car insurance premium, the property tax bill, and somebody's root canal all show up together. Four months of "on plan," one month of carnage, and the quiet conclusion that budgeting doesn't work for your life.

I've watched this happen to careful people — spreadsheet people, people who genuinely track things. And I built budgets that failed this exact way myself, for years, before I understood what was actually wrong.

The budget wasn't broken. The frame was.

## Spending isn't monthly. Budgets pretend it is.

When the JPMorgan Chase Institute studied millions of real bank accounts, it found that nearly every household's spending moves more than 5 percent month to month — and for the middle half of households, the swings run to roughly 25 percent in either direction. Nobody spends the same in February as in August. Nobody.

And most of that swing isn't impulse. It's *scheduled* money on a lumpy calendar: semi-annual premiums, annual registrations, quarterly bills, a December that reliably costs double a May.

A budget that resets every 30 days has no memory. It can't tell the difference between overspending and an annual bill arriving exactly on schedule. So March looks like a failure. Every year. By design.

## The fix is older than the apps

Businesses figured this out a century ago: you budget the year, then break it into months. The annual frame absorbs the lumps; the monthly view keeps you honest week to week.

This week's post on the blog is the full system for doing that with irregular expenses — the five steps, briefly:

**Find them all.** Pull 12 months of transactions, not three, and list everything that doesn't bill monthly: premiums, taxes, car costs, home repairs, medical, gifts, travel, annual renewals. Memory is not enough here. In one C+R Research survey, people guessed their subscriptions cost $86 a month; the actual average was $219.

**Annualize, then divide by 12.** A $1,200 premium isn't a January problem, it's $100 a month that happens to be collected once. Sinking funds, if you like the classic name.

**Budget on a 12-month frame.** Put the money on the year, shape it into the months where it actually bills. Now a lumpy month shows *on plan* instead of blown.

**Check the cash, not just the math.** If two big bills share November, your average month can be fine while November goes negative. Look at the actual calendar of when money leaves and fix the thin months while they're still months away.

**Review quarterly.** Premiums drift up. Renewals sneak in. Fifteen minutes, four times a year.

## Why I care about this one

Of everything I've built into Tally, the annual budget is the piece I'm most stubborn about, because it's the piece that made budgeting finally stick for me. Tally budgets the year with monthly granularity, tracks actuals against it, and its cash planning flags shortfall months before they arrive. But honestly — the frame matters more than the tool. If you do nothing else this week, pull your last 12 months of transactions and add up what didn't bill monthly. The number usually changes how people think about their "monthly" budget on the spot.

One more thing the research made me sit with: in the Federal Reserve's latest household survey, only 63 percent of adults could cover a $400 surprise with cash or its equivalent. Some of that is income. But some of it is that we file predictable-but-irregular costs under "surprise" — and you can't save for a surprise. Name the category, and it stops being one.

Your spending doesn't happen 30 days at a time. Your budget shouldn't either.
