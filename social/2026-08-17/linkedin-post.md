Most budgets don't fail on discipline. They fail on design.

Here's the pattern I see constantly: four months "on plan," then car insurance, property taxes, and a vet bill land in the same week. The month looks like a disaster, the person concludes budgeting doesn't work, and the spreadsheet goes quiet until January.

But look at the data. JPMorgan Chase Institute studied millions of bank accounts and found nearly every household's spending swings more than 5% month to month — with typical swings around 25% in either direction. A budget that resets every 30 days has no memory. It literally cannot tell overspending apart from an annual bill arriving on schedule.

Businesses solved this a century ago: budget the year, break it into months. The annual frame absorbs the lumps. The monthly view keeps you honest.

That's the frame I built Tally's budgeting on, and it's the subject of this week's post — a five-step system for irregular expenses: find them in 12 months of transactions, annualize and divide by 12, budget on an annual frame, check the actual cash calendar for thin months, review quarterly.

One number from the research stuck with me: people in one survey guessed they spent $86 a month on subscriptions. The actual average was $219.
