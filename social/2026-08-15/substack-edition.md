# The most expensive dollar in early retirement

Last week I wrote about the gap years — that stretch between the last paycheck and Medicare when your tax rate goes on sale. A few readers wrote back with some version of the same question: "What about health insurance?"

Fair. Because for anyone retiring before 65, the cheapest tax years of your life are also the years you're buying your own coverage on the marketplace. And in 2026, those two facts just collided hard.

Here's what changed. The enhanced ACA subsidies passed in 2021 — the ones that removed the income cap — expired on December 31, 2025. The old 400%-of-poverty-level cliff is back. And it returned in a year when insurers raised marketplace premiums about 26% on average; KFF reports the average monthly premium payment jumped 58% this year, from $113 to $178.

The line for 2026: about $62,600 of income for a single person, $84,600 for a couple, in the lower 48. Under it, your benchmark silver premium is capped at no more than 9.96% of income — for that couple, roughly $8,400 a year, maximum. One dollar over, and the cap simply vanishes. Full sticker price, which for a couple in their early sixties is routinely a multiple of that.

One dollar of income. Thousands of dollars of consequence. It's the most expensive dollar in early retirement.

This week's post on the blog is the playbook — but I want to flag the part that matters most if you read last week's edition, because the two collide directly.

Last week's math said a married couple can show $133,000 of gross income in 2026 before any dollar gets taxed above 12%, and that gap years are the time to fill that room with Roth conversions. All still true — after 65, when Medicare has replaced the marketplace.

Before 65, on an ACA plan, the binding number isn't $133,000. It's $84,600. Convert to the top of the 12% bracket and you blow through the cliff, and the cost of crossing isn't 12% — it's 12% plus the entire lost subsidy, landed on the handful of dollars that pushed you over.

The way through is choosing which years are for which job. Either run subsidy-first — keep your income (MAGI) comfortably under the line during ACA years and save the conversions for the 65-to-RMD window — or, if the conversion math is compelling, bunch it: one deliberate over-the-cliff year with a big conversion, surrounded by tight, fully subsidized years. What doesn't work is drifting slightly over the line every year and paying full freight annually for small conversions.

The good news buried in all this: unlike a paycheck, retirement income is chosen. The cliff is measured against MAGI, and MAGI is a dial. Roth withdrawals don't count. Spending cash doesn't count. Selling investments only counts the gain, not the proceeds. A couple spending $90,000 a year can show $60,000 of MAGI or $110,000 depending entirely on which accounts the money comes from. That's not a loophole — it's the system working as designed, and it's the whole game of the gap years.
