# The rule that launched a thousand retirements has a horizon problem

I retired a spreadsheet habit before I built one into an app, so believe me when I say I understand the appeal of the 4% rule. One number. One multiple. Save 25x your spending and you are free. I have run that calculation on napkins, whiteboards, and at least one boarding pass.

But this week I went back to the research, and the deeper you dig, the clearer the problem gets: the 4% rule answers a question most early retirees are not asking.

Bill Bengen's original 1994 study — the source of the rule — tested whether a portfolio survives 30 years. His worst-case historical retiree, the poor soul who quit work in October 1968 into a wall of bad returns and inflation, still made it 30 years withdrawing 4.15% a year, inflation-adjusted. Hence: 4%.

Retire at 45, though, and you are not planning a 30-year retirement. You are planning 50. And at 50 or 60 years, the historical math turns noticeably less friendly. Karsten Jeske at Early Retirement Now backtested it: over 60 years, 4% succeeded 89% of the time with 100% stocks — and only 65% of the time at 50% stocks. His conclusion for very long retirements is blunt: the safe rate is not 4%.

Here is the part I find almost funny. While the FIRE community argues about whether 4% is too aggressive for long horizons, the experts cannot even agree on the original 30-year question anymore:

Bengen published a new book in 2025 revising his own number up to 4.7%, based on a broader portfolio with small caps and international stocks. Morningstar's latest State of Retirement Income research, using forward-looking return projections instead of history, says new retirees should start at 3.9% for 2026.

4.7% versus 3.9% — nearly a full point apart, from two of the most credible sources in the field, both modeling the same 30-year retirement. They are not contradicting each other so much as answering different questions with different assumptions. Which is exactly the point: the "right" withdrawal rate was never a universal constant. It depends on your portfolio, your horizon, your taxes, your other income, and what the market does in your first decade.

A single percentage cannot see that you will pull from a brokerage account first and pay capital gains rates, or that a Roth conversion at 52 could cut your lifetime tax bill, or that Social Security lands in year 20 of your plan and changes everything after it, or that your ACA premium depends on how your income lands against subsidy thresholds. Two households with identical portfolios can get very different outcomes on taxes alone.

And the horizon compounds all of it. The failure mode in every backtest is the same: bad returns early, while withdrawals keep carving into principal. A 30-year retirement gives that pattern fewer chances to appear and less time to finish the job. A 50-year retirement gives it both. That is why the question "is 4% safe?" has no single answer — the same rate can be conservative for a 65-year-old and optimistic for a 45-year-old with the same portfolio.

So my advice, as someone who spent years doing this in spreadsheets: use 25x as a milestone, not a finish line. Then replace the rule with your own numbers. Project your actual accounts and actual spending with taxes modeled year by year. Stress-test the plan against thousands of market sequences, not one average line. And test the levers you control — one more year of work, a spending floor in down years, a conversion window before Social Security.
