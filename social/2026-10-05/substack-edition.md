# The Question Every Budget Eventually Asks

Years ago, before Tally existed, I had a budgeting app I liked and a retirement spreadsheet I feared. The app knew everything about my Tuesdays. The spreadsheet held the only question that actually kept me up at night — *am I going to be okay?* — and the two had never met. Every few months I'd copy a spending number from one into the other, round it to something flattering, and call it planning.

That gap is the reason I get one particular email over and over, and lately it comes with the same app name in it: people shopping for a Monarch Money alternative, asking what I'd look for.

So this week I wrote the comparison post I'd want to read, disclosure included — I make a competing app, so every claim in it is specific and checkable. The full version is here:

**[Monarch Money Alternatives in 2026: What to Check Before You Switch](https://thetally.io/blog/monarch-money-alternatives-2026)**

Here's the short version, and the part I care most about.

First: Monarch is a good app. The connections work, the interface is clean, sharing with a partner is done right. Nobody should switch out of spite. What sends people shopping is usually price structure — budgeting is $99.99 a year, but retirement forecasting sits in a Plus tier at $199.99 — or the moment they realize the plan they're on tracks the past beautifully and says nothing about the future.

Second: before comparing apps, decide which problem you're solving. Budgeting apps answer "where did this month go." Retirement planners answer "when can I stop." They're different species, and most disappointment comes from buying one and expecting the other. The interesting category is the small one that does both — where your real spending feeds your long-term forecast automatically, instead of through a number you typed in from memory.

Third, and this is the one I'd put on a billboard if billboards took footnotes: **read how any forecasting tool handles taxes.** Monarch's own documentation describes flat tax-rate assumptions you pick yourself — 15% on capital gains, 20% on tax-deferred withdrawals, by default. Flat rates are honest simplifications, but retirement is precisely where they break. Real withdrawals fill real brackets. RMDs shove you into brackets you didn't choose. States take anywhere from nothing to double digits. And the entire logic of a Roth conversion lives in the gap between your bracket now and your bracket at 73 — a gap that doesn't exist in a flat-rate world.

A flat-rate forecast isn't wrong, exactly. It's confident in a way it hasn't earned. If most of your savings sit in tax-deferred accounts, the tax math isn't a detail of your plan. It more or less *is* your plan.

This is the hill Tally was built on, so you know where I stand: federal brackets, all 50 states, FICA, NIIT, RMDs at the SECURE 2.0 ages, Social Security taxation, Roth conversion analysis — inside the forecast, running against your actual spending, with Monte Carlo on top because one projected future is a bedtime story and a thousand of them is a plan.

If you're happily budgeting and the long-term question hasn't started itching yet, keep what you have. Truly. An app you'll open every week beats a theoretically better one you won't.

But if you're in the shopping mood anyway, the post walks through the six things to check — where the forecast gets its spending number, taxes, Monte Carlo, couples modeling, the import path, the all-in price — plus a short guide to switching without losing your history. (Rule one: export your CSV *before* you cancel anything. Reading your own past shouldn't be a race against a billing date.)

And if you decide the both-in-one category is where you belong, Tally imports Monarch's export directly, and YNAB's too. The whole app — budgeting and the lifetime forecast together — is $79 a year, which I'll just note is less than either of the two subscriptions it replaces.

One app should be able to know your Tuesdays and your eighties. That's the whole bet.
