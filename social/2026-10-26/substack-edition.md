# The withdrawal order everyone repeats (and what it costs)

I had a conversation last week that I can't stop thinking about.

A reader — early 60s, done well, careful saver — walked me through his retirement plan. Brokerage account first, he said. Then the IRA. Roth last. He'd read it in three different places, so it had to be right.

Then he showed me his projected tax returns for the next ten years. For the first eight, he'd owe almost nothing. He was proud of that. I winced, because I knew what year nine looked like.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about those zero-tax years: they're not free. They're expensive.

## The use-it-or-lose-it problem

In 2026, a married couple filing jointly gets a $32,200 standard deduction. If you're both 65 or older, a new senior deduction adds $6,000 apiece through 2028. That's $44,200 of income the IRS would tax at exactly 0% — but only if you have income for it to erase.

Live entirely off your brokerage account and your taxable income is close to nothing. The deduction has nothing to erase. It expires December 31 and a fresh one arrives in January, and if you waste that one too, nobody sends you a refund for it.

Meanwhile, the IRA you're dutifully preserving keeps growing. And every dollar in it is a dollar of future ordinary income with an appointment on the calendar: required minimum distributions, now starting at 73 (or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later).

My reader's $900,000 IRA, untouched until 73, becomes roughly $1.4 million at 6% growth. First RMD: somewhere around $54,000, whether he needs it or not, stacked on top of Social Security — up to 85% of which can be taxable. The man who paid nothing for eight years gets to spend the rest of his retirement in the 22% bracket or above.

He didn't avoid the tax. He compressed it into the years when he'd have the least control over it.

## Flip the question

The better question isn't "which account first?" It's "how much income can I recognize this year at rates I'll never see again?"

For that couple, the answer in 2026 looks something like: pull about $44,000 from the IRA (federal tax: roughly zero, thanks to those deductions), then cover the rest of the year's spending from the brokerage account — where long-term gains are taxed at 0% until taxable income crosses $98,900 for joint filers.

Same spending. Dramatically different lifetime tax bill. The low brackets get used every single year instead of wasted for a decade and then blown through.

And for married couples there's a second reason to drain traditional balances sooner: eventually one of you will file single. Half the deduction, compressed brackets, usually most of the same portfolio. The wide married brackets are a window, not a permanent feature.

One more thing, because someone always asks: this doesn't demote the Roth. Saving it for last is usually still right. But it's also your release valve — in a year when one more dollar of IRA income would push Social Security into taxation or spill gains out of the 0% zone, covering the gap from the Roth holds your taxable income exactly where you want it. A Roth you never touch is a parking spot. A Roth you deploy at the right moment is a steering wheel.
