# The question your budgeting app can't answer

A reader emailed me a version of a question I've heard dozens of times now: "I've used YNAB faithfully for six years. I know exactly what we spend. So why do I still have no idea when I can retire?"

Six years of perfect data, and no answer to the only question that data exists to serve.

I don't think that's the user's failure, and I don't think it's exactly YNAB's either. YNAB set out to run your month, and it runs your month well. But somewhere in your forties or fifties, the question changes shape. It stops being "can we afford this" and becomes "are we going to be okay." And a budgeting app — any budgeting-only app — goes quiet right at that moment.

This week's post on the blog is about that wall, and what to look for when you've hit it.

Here's the mechanical heart of the problem. In YNAB, your 401(k), IRA, and brokerage accounts are what their docs call tracking accounts. One number each: the balance. No holdings, no returns, no model of what those balances become when you stop contributing and start withdrawing. Your retirement — the largest financial project of your life — sits in your budgeting app as a line that goes up.

So people bolt on a second tool. A retirement planner for the thirty-year picture, YNAB for the daily one. I ran a version of this setup myself for years, and it has a flaw nobody warns you about: the two apps never talk. The planner needs your annual spending as an input, so you type in an estimate — a guess at the exact number your budgeting app tracks to the penny. Then your spending drifts, the way spending does, and the plan quietly stops being true. You're paying for two subscriptions, and the expensive one is running on a stale copy of data the cheap one has in real time.

Your spending history is the single best input a retirement forecast can have. That's the whole reason Tally exists as one app instead of two: the transactions that run your November budget are the same ones that run your lifetime forecast — federal and state taxes, Social Security timing, Roth conversions, RMDs, Monte Carlo, down to a runway date you can watch move when your life changes.

Two things from it worth repeating here.

First, the honest part: if envelope budgeting is the ritual that keeps your household on track — if giving every dollar a job is the point — stay with YNAB for that. Tally doesn't imitate the envelope method, and I'd rather lose you to the right tool than win you to the wrong one. The time to move is when you keep asking a question your current app can't answer.

Second, the switching cost is smaller than you think. The real lock-in with a budgeting app was never the subscription; it's the years of categorized history. Tally imports your YNAB data, so the record comes with you. The price works in your favor too — YNAB is $109 a year now, Tally is $79, and the $79 covers both halves of the job.

A thing I believe after years in these numbers: a budget that can't see past the end of the month eventually demoralizes you. You do the work, you categorize, you stay under on groceries — and nothing tells you that it's adding up to freedom. Connecting the daily data to the lifetime picture is what makes the daily discipline feel worth it. You skip the impulse purchase and you can literally watch your runway move.

That's the answer I'd give that reader. Six years of YNAB data isn't wasted. It's the first half of a plan that's been waiting for its second half.
