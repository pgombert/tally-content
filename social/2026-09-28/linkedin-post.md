Here's a stat that explains a lot of budgeting frustration: 42.2% of American private businesses pay their people every two weeks. It's the most common pay schedule in the country, per the BLS.

Now count the budgeting tools built around a 14-day pay cycle. Nearly all of them assume monthly income, because bills are monthly. So the most common paycheck in America gets budgeted on a grid it doesn't fit.

The math is the tell: 26 paychecks, 12 months. Twice a year, a month contains three paydays. If your checks land October 2, 16, and 30, that's next month for you.

Most people treat that third check as a windfall, and windfalls get absorbed. The fix I keep coming back to is boring and it works: build your baseline budget on 24 checks so every month is predictable, give each paycheck a defined job, and write a standing order for the two extras before they arrive — emergency fund, card balance, or straight into investments.

Building Tally, this shaped a real design decision: cash planning that projects balances payday by payday, not month by month, and flags the exact date a shortfall would hit. Because the month is an accounting fiction. The payday is real.

If your October has three paychecks in it, decide this week what the third one is for.
