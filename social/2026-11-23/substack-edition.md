# The day your retirement quietly funds itself

A few years ago I was talking with a friend who was grinding through a job he didn't like because "I have to keep maxing the 401(k)." We ran his numbers on a napkin. He didn't have to. He'd crossed the line about two years earlier and never noticed.

That line has a name in the FIRE community: Coast FIRE. It's the point where your existing retirement savings, left alone, will compound their way to a fully funded retirement by your target date — no further contributions required. You still work. You still earn. But you're working to fund your life, not your entire future. That's a different job, even when it's the same job.

This week's post on the blog walks through the full calculation. Here's the short version.

**Start at the end.** How much do you need on the day you retire? The common shortcut is 25x your expected annual spending — the 4% rule run backward. Spend $60,000 a year, and that's $1.5 million in today's dollars.

**Discount it back to today.** Your Coast FIRE number is that retirement number divided by (1 + growth rate) raised to the years you have left. Use a real, after-inflation rate — around 5% is a common assumption for a stock-heavy portfolio, and conservative planners go lower.

**See what the table says.** Targeting $1.5 million at 65, assuming 5% real growth: at 35 you need about $347,000 invested today. At 45, about $565,000. At 55, about $921,000. Same destination, wildly different price — because what you're really buying is years of compounding, and they get more expensive as the supply runs out.

That table is the most motivating thing I know in personal finance. A 45-year-old with $565,000 has already done the hard part. Everything after that is letting it grow.

But here's where I'll be honest about the napkin math, because this is a financial product and accuracy beats cleverness: the one-line formula is a single straight line through an uncertain world. One growth rate. One spending guess. No taxes. Before you act on it — before you take the lower-stress job or stop contributing — it deserves a real stress test.

Four things the simple formula ignores:

Markets don't return their average every year. A Monte Carlo simulation runs your plan through thousands of market sequences and tells you how often it fails, not just whether it works on average.

Taxes. $1.5 million in a traditional 401(k) is not $1.5 million you can spend. The same balance split differently across pre-tax, Roth, and taxable accounts supports meaningfully different retirements.

Social Security. Most Coast FIRE math ignores it entirely, which makes the target harder than it needs to be. Even a partial benefit changes what your portfolio has to carry.

Your actual spending — including healthcare for any years before Medicare at 65, which is the number early retirees most often get wrong.
