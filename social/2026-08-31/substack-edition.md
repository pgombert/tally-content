# Your budget app has never met your retirement plan

I counted my own tabs this week. Before I built Tally, my financial life lived in four places: a budgeting app that knew everything about my Tuesdays, a retirement spreadsheet that knew everything about my sixties, a brokerage login, and a folder of tax PDFs. Four tools, zero conversations between them. My budget app had never met my retirement plan. They didn't even know the other existed.

If that sounds familiar, it's not a personal failing. It's how the market is built.

When Mint shut down in early 2024, the replacements split into two camps. One camp handles today's money: YNAB, Monarch. They sync your accounts, categorize your spending, and tell you what happened this month. The other camp handles future money: Boldin, ProjectionLab. They model your next thirty years, run Monte Carlo simulations, and tell you whether the whole thing works. Each camp is good. Neither can do the other's job — and one of them, ProjectionLab, says so plainly: it's a planner, not a budgeting app. It won't even connect to your bank.

This week on the blog I priced the split out properly. At current list prices, YNAB plus Boldin runs $253 a year. Monarch plus ProjectionLab is about $229. So the going rate for a full picture of your money is somewhere around $230 to $250 a year, paid to two companies whose products don't share a single byte of data.

The subscriptions aren't the real cost, though. The real cost is what happens in the gap between the two apps.

Your retirement forecast has one input that matters more than every other: what you actually spend. And in a two-app world, that number gets typed into your planner by hand, from memory, usually optimistically. Meanwhile your budget app is quietly recording the truth. Nobody reconciles them. If your guess is off by $800 a month — and most guesses are off by more — your projection is wrong by hundreds of thousands of dollars over a retirement. Not because the planner's math is bad. Because it was fed a fiction.

Then the plans go stale. Updating a manual planner is a chore, so it slides from monthly to quarterly to "I should really look at that." The budget stays fresh because it updates itself. The plan — the thing that answers the only question that keeps people up at night — drifts.

The full post walks through what a single app would need to do to honestly replace the pair: synced accounts, real budgets with budget-versus-actuals, a lifetime forecast that takes taxes seriously (federal and state, RMDs, Roth math), scenarios and Monte Carlo, and — the part that matters most — the loop between them, where your actual spending feeds your forecast without you doing anything.

It also includes the section I most wanted to write: when you should keep two apps. If YNAB's envelope method is what keeps your spending honest, keep YNAB — the method is worth more than the overlap. If you won't link a bank account anywhere on principle, ProjectionLab's manual entry is a feature. I'd rather you have the right stack than my stack.

But if you're paying two subscriptions because that's just how things landed after Mint died, and your plan hasn't been opened since spring, the split is costing you twice.
