# The $1,080-a-month decision most people make on gut feel

Hi — Pete here.

There's a moment in every retirement plan where the spreadsheet goes quiet and a single question sits there waiting: when do I claim Social Security?

You'd think a decision this big would come with good tooling. Mostly it comes with folklore. "Take it at 62 before the program goes broke." "Always wait until 70, the math says so." Both camps are confident. Both are skipping the actual arithmetic.

So this week I wrote up the real numbers, current for 2026. The short version:

**The spread is enormous.** If you were born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. Claim at 62 and you get 70% of your benefit — permanently. Wait until 70 and delayed credits take you to 124%. The age-70 check is about 77% larger than the age-62 check. On a $2,000 full benefit, that's $1,400 a month versus $2,480 — a $1,080-a-month gap for life.

**The breakeven lands around 80.** Claim early and you bank five to eight years of checks before the late claimer catches up, somewhere in your late 70s. If you're confident you won't see 80, early claiming wins on paper. But half of people outlive the averages, and the miserable scenario isn't dying with money left — it's living to 95 without it. A bigger check is longevity insurance you can't buy this cheap anywhere else.

**Couples change everything.** When one spouse dies, the smaller check stops and the survivor keeps the larger one. The higher earner's claiming age sets the survivor's income for potentially decades. "When should I claim?" is usually the wrong question. "When should each of us claim?" is the right one.

**Working makes early claiming mostly pointless.** In 2026, claim before full retirement age while earning more than $24,480 and Social Security withholds $1 for every $2 over the limit. You get it credited back later, but if you're still working at 63, there's often little reason to claim.

**Taxes are the quiet variable.** Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable, the thresholds haven't moved since the Reagan era, and a big IRA withdrawal can drag more of your benefit into taxable income. Meanwhile, through 2028 there's an extra $6,000-per-person deduction for people 65 and up that softens the hit for many middle-income retirees. Claiming age, withdrawals, and Roth conversions all interact — which is why a breakeven chart alone can't settle this.
