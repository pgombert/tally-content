# The $500,000 Number From 1997

In 1997, Congress decided how much home-sale profit you could keep tax-free: $250,000 if you're single, $500,000 if you're married.

That was almost thirty years ago. Titanic was in theaters. Home prices have multiplied since. The caps haven't moved a dollar — they were never indexed to inflation.

I think about this a lot, because the house is the biggest number in most people's financial lives — bigger than the 401(k) for a large share of the people Tally is built for. And when you sell it, you trigger the largest single taxable transaction you'll ever make. Most people do it exactly once, with no practice run.

So this week's post is a plain-English walkthrough of how a home sale is actually taxed in 2026. Here's the short version.

**The exclusion does most of the work.** Own and live in your main home for 2 of the last 5 years, and you can exclude up to $250,000 of gain (single) or $500,000 (married filing jointly). For most sellers, that's the whole story: no federal tax, done.

**But "gain" is smaller than you think — if you kept receipts.** Your taxable gain isn't sale price minus 1998 purchase price. Selling costs come off the top. And every capital improvement you ever made — roof, kitchen, addition, HVAC — adds to your basis. In the worked example in the post, $80,000 of saved receipts erases $80,000 of taxable gain. The most boring folder in your file cabinet might be worth five figures.

**The part above the cap is long-term capital gain.** For 2026 that means 0%, 15%, or 20%, and the gain stacks on top of your other income. A married couple keeps paying 0% until taxable income passes $98,900 — so a modest-income couple with a gain slightly over the cap can owe far less than they fear. There's a full example with real numbers in the post.

**The traps are about timing, not rates.** Three stood out as I wrote this one:

If you lose your spouse, you keep the full $500,000 exclusion only if you sell within two years. After that it drops to $250,000. It's a brutal deadline to put on a grieving person, and almost nobody warns them.

Medicare premiums are set by your income from two years earlier. A big gain in 2026 can mean a premium surcharge in 2028. Temporary, but real, and it belongs in the decision.

And the 3.8% net investment income tax kicks in above $200,000/$250,000 of income — thresholds that, like the exclusion itself, are frozen forever. Inflation does the tightening automatically.

**One deadline-saver worth knowing.** If life forces a sale before you hit the two-year mark — a job move, health, certain unforeseen events — you don't lose the break entirely. You get a prorated share of the cap, months-in-the-house divided by 24. Eleven qualifying months still gets a couple roughly $229,000 of exclusion. Enough, usually, to cover the gain on a short stay.

And a practical note on receipts, since several of you will be hunting for 25-year-old paperwork: you're looking for capital improvements, not repairs. Roof, remodel, addition, new systems, landscaping that stays. Permits, contractor invoices, even old card statements can substantiate it. One folder, one evening. Future-you, sitting across from a closing table, will be grateful.

**The real lesson is lead time.** A house sale is the rare tax event you can see coming years in advance. Which year you sell, what else you do that year — Roth conversions, big withdrawals — and whether you can document your basis can swing the outcome by thousands.
