# The wall at 59½ has four doors

The most expensive sentence in early-retirement planning might be "my money is locked up until 59½." I've heard it from people with seven-figure 401(k)s who kept working years they didn't want to work, because they believed the wall was solid. It isn't. The 10% early-withdrawal penalty has four legal exits, all of them printed in the tax code, and this week's post on the blog walks through every one. Here's the short version.

**Door 1 is the rule of 55.** Leave your job — quit, laid off, doesn't matter — during or after the calendar year you turn 55, and you can tap that employer's 401(k) with no penalty. Public safety workers get it at 50, or after 25 years of service. Two catches. It only covers the plan of the job you just left, which sets up the cruelest own-goal in personal finance: rolling that 401(k) into an IRA "to simplify" and locking the door behind you. And the IRS allowing these withdrawals doesn't mean your plan does — some only permit a lump sum. One phone call to your administrator settles it: "Do you allow partial withdrawals after separation?"

**Door 2 is 72(t)**, the substantially-equal-payments rule. Commit to a fixed annual draw from your IRA, calculated one of three IRS-approved ways, and the penalty disappears at any age. Since the IRS put a 5% floor under the calculation rate in 2022, the numbers are respectable: a 50-year-old with a $500,000 IRA can draw about $30,100 a year under the amortization method. The teeth: you're locked in for the longer of five years or until 59½ — start at 50 and that's nine and a half years — and breaking the schedule by a single dollar triggers the penalty retroactively on everything you've taken, plus interest. It's a precision tool. Measure twice.

**Door 3 is the Roth conversion ladder**, the FIRE community's favorite. Each year, convert a year of future spending from your traditional IRA to your Roth, paying tax at your newly-low retirement rates. Five tax years later, that conversion can come out penalty-free. Do it annually and you've built a conveyor belt. The catch is the bridge — you need five years of spending from somewhere else while the first rungs mature — and the conversions themselves are taxable income, which can quietly eat your ACA subsidy if you're not watching.

**Door 4 almost nobody mentions: the governmental 457(b).** If you work for a state or local government, distributions after you separate carry no 10% penalty at all. Any age. If you have one, it's your first-out money, and a lot of people holding these plans have no idea.

Here's what I actually want you to take from this. The penalty is the easy part. Any of these doors avoids it. The hard part is that every door does the same thing — turns pre-tax money into taxable income — and so they all compete with each other, with your tax brackets, with your health-insurance subsidy, and with the Roth conversions you might rather be doing. Whether to start a 72(t) at 50 or bridge on taxable savings until a ladder matures isn't a rule of thumb question. It depends on your balances, your state, your spending, and about a dozen thresholds that move every January.
