# Substack edition — 2026-09-21

**Subject line:** The rule that just took away your pre-tax catch-up

---

I got my first note about this one from a reader in July, before the rule even kicked in. His payroll department had sent a two-line email saying his 401(k) catch-up contributions would be "recharacterized as Roth effective January 1," and he wanted to know if he'd done something wrong.

He hadn't. Congress did it to him, back in 2022, with a delay long enough that almost everyone forgot it was coming.

Here's the short version. Since January 1, if you're 50 or older and your FICA wages from your employer were over $150,000 in 2025, every catch-up dollar you put into that employer's 401(k), 403(b), or governmental 457(b) has to be a Roth contribution. After-tax. No pre-tax option. The IRS finalized the regulations last September, and 2026 is the first year it's real.

Your regular contributions — the first $24,500 under the new 2026 limit — are untouched. This is only about the catch-up: $8,000 if you're 50 or older, or $11,250 if you turn 60, 61, 62, or 63 this year. That larger number is its own underused story, by the way. The window lasts four calendar years and then closes at 64.

What does the rule actually cost? The deduction. An $8,000 pre-tax catch-up used to knock $1,920 off your federal bill if you're in the 24% bracket, $2,560 in the 32%. That saving is gone, and it comes out of your 2026 paycheck withholding, which is why I'd check your withholding this month rather than discovering the gap in April.

Now the part the angry headlines skip: what you get back.

Roth dollars grow tax-free forever. They carry no required minimum distributions — since 2024, Roth 401(k) balances are exempt from RMDs entirely, while your pre-tax balance faces forced withdrawals starting at 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. And every Roth dollar is a hedge against the bet buried inside all pre-tax saving: that your tax rate in retirement will be lower than it is today. For people who've saved well — which is exactly who this rule targets — that bet is often weaker than they think. Large pre-tax balances plus RMDs plus Social Security can add up to retirement brackets that look uncomfortably like working-year brackets.

So my honest read is that this rule takes away a choice and charges you a real cost today, in exchange for pushing you toward something a lot of 55-year-old high savers should have been doing with at least part of their catch-up anyway.

Two warnings, though.

First, check that your plan actually offers Roth catch-ups. Under the final regulations, if your plan has no Roth option, affected high earners can't make catch-up contributions *at all* until the plan adds one. That's an $8,000-a-year hole in your savings plan hiding behind one unasked question to HR.

Second, don't take my generic read as your answer. Whether forced Roth is a mild tax or a quiet favor depends entirely on the gap between your bracket now and your bracket in retirement — and that second number depends on your pre-tax balance, your state, your Social Security timing, and the RMD schedule. It's a forecast, not a slogan.
