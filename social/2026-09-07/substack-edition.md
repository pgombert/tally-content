# The tax bracket with a zero printed on it

A few years ago, someone I respect told me the smartest tax move he ever made was selling a fund he had no intention of getting rid of. He sold it in the morning, bought it back after lunch, and paid nothing in federal tax on the gain. I remember thinking that couldn't be right. It was right. I've been quietly evangelizing the 0% capital gains bracket ever since.

This week's post on the blog is the full walkthrough for 2026, and I want to give you the short version here, because the numbers this year are better than most people realize.

The 0% rate on long-term capital gains applies as long as your taxable income stays under $49,450 (single) or $98,900 (married filing jointly) in 2026. Taxable income — after deductions, but including the gains themselves. That last clause is the part people trip on, so here's the stacking rule in one sentence: your gains sit on top of your ordinary income, and only the slice that pokes above the threshold gets taxed.

Because the threshold is taxable income, deductions raise the real ceiling. A couple filing jointly gets a $32,200 standard deduction in 2026. If they're both 65 or older, the new senior deduction adds up to $6,000 each through 2028. Run it all out: a retired couple whose only income is long-term gains could realize $143,100 in 2026 and owe zero federal income tax. Not deferred. Zero.

If you're in the FIRE crowd or in the gap years between your last paycheck and Social Security, this is your bracket. Those low-ordinary-income years are exactly when it's widest.

The move it enables is called tax-gain harvesting, and it's almost anticlimactic: sell the winner, realize the gain at 0%, buy it back the same day if you want. The wash-sale rule only applies to losses — there's no waiting period for gains. What you walk away with is a stepped-up cost basis. The gain you harvested at 0% is out of the ledger forever. Do this for a few years in a row and you can move six figures of embedded gains out of your portfolio at a lifetime tax rate of zero.

So why doesn't everyone do it? Mostly because the traps are real and they live outside the tax return:

The gain still counts as income for everything else. If you're under 65 buying insurance on an ACA exchange, a "tax-free" harvest raises the MAGI that sets your subsidy, and the clawback can swamp the tax savings. Past 63, this year's income sets your Medicare premiums two years out. Most states tax the gain no matter what the federal bracket says. And a Roth conversion competes for exactly the same bracket space — in most low-income years you get to do one of those well, not both.

None of this makes the bracket less valuable. It makes it a December problem instead of an April problem. The whole game is knowing your taxable income before the year closes — while you can still act — instead of finding out at filing time that you left $70,000 of 0% room on the table.
