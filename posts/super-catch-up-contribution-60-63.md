Most retirement rules punish you for waiting. This one rewards you for four years, then quietly stops.

Buried in SECURE 2.0 is a provision that lets workers aged 60 through 63 contribute meaningfully more to a workplace retirement plan than anyone else — including people older than them. It is often called the "super catch-up." It is not a bonus, not a credit, and not something your payroll system will suggest. It is a door that opens the year you turn 60 and closes the year you turn 64.

The 2027 numbers just came out, and they make the gap wider than it has ever been.

## What the 2027 limits actually say

The IRS published the 2027 retirement plan limits in Notice 2026-66 on November 12, 2026. Three numbers matter here:

- **Employee deferral limit (401(k), 403(b), most 457(b), TSP): $25,500**, up from $24,500 in 2026.
- **Standard catch-up, ages 50–59 and 64+: $8,500**, up from $8,000.
- **"Super" catch-up, ages 60–63: $11,750**, up from $11,250 in 2026.

Stack those up and the picture gets concrete. A 58-year-old can defer up to $34,000 of their own money in 2027. A 61-year-old can defer up to $37,250. A 65-year-old is back down to $34,000.

That is $3,250 a year of extra tax-advantaged room, available for four calendar years and no more. Use all four and you have moved $13,000 of additional deferrals — plus whatever it earns — out of a taxable account and into a retirement plan.

## The eligibility rule is simpler than people assume

You qualify based on the age you reach by December 31 of that year, not your age on January 1 and not your birthday month.

If you turn 60 in November 2027, you are eligible for the full $11,750 super catch-up for all of 2027. If you turn 64 in December 2027, you are not eligible at all that year — you are back to the standard $8,500, even for the eleven months you were 63.

So the window is exactly four calendar years: the year you turn 60, 61, 62, and 63. For someone born in 1967, that is 2027 through 2030. For someone born in 1964, 2027 is the last year — and 2028 is a step backward of $3,250 in available room.

That last part catches people. Nearly every other retirement threshold in the tax code moves in one direction as you age. This one reverses.

## The catch that actually matters: your plan has to offer it

The super catch-up is optional for employers. SECURE 2.0 permits plans to offer it; it does not require them to.

If your plan does not include the provision, your contribution election will simply cap at the standard catch-up and you may never see an error message explaining why. The money you thought was going in stays in your paycheck, gets taxed, and lands in a taxable account instead.

This is a five-minute check. Log into your plan portal and look at the contribution limit it displays for your age, or email HR and ask one question: "Does our plan offer the age 60–63 catch-up contribution under SECURE 2.0 Section 109?" Do it before January payroll locks, not in December when you are trying to true up.

## The second catch: for higher earners, it has to be Roth

SECURE 2.0 also requires that catch-up contributions be made as designated Roth contributions if your prior-year wages from that employer exceeded an indexed threshold. For catch-up contributions made in 2027, the threshold is **$155,000 of 2026 Social Security (FICA) wages from the employer sponsoring the plan** — up from $150,000 of 2025 wages for 2026 catch-ups.

Two details people get wrong:

**It is wages from that specific employer**, not your household income and not your total AGI. Two spouses each earning $120,000 are both below the line. One person earning $160,000 at one job is above it.

**It uses last year's wages.** Your 2027 catch-up treatment was determined by what you earned in 2026. Nothing you do in 2027 changes it.

If you are above the threshold, your $11,750 goes in after tax and comes out tax-free later. That is not a penalty — for a lot of people in their early 60s it is the better outcome anyway — but it changes the math. You lose the current-year deduction, which means your taxable income in your highest-earning years is higher than you may have projected. If you were counting on that deduction to stay under an ACA subsidy threshold or below an IRMAA tier, that assumption needs rechecking.

## Where it sits relative to the other caps

Two quick clarifications, because this is where plan statements confuse people:

The total additions limit — everything you and your employer put in, including match and profit sharing — is **$75,000 for 2027**, up from $72,000. Catch-up contributions sit on top of that cap, not inside it. So an eligible 62-year-old could theoretically see $86,750 total land in the account in one year.

And none of this touches your IRA. The IRA contribution limit stays at **$7,500 for 2027**, unchanged from 2026, with a $1,100 catch-up at 50 and older. There is no age 60–63 enhancement on the IRA side. That is a workplace-plan-only provision.

## Whether you should actually use it

Having the room is not the same as it being the right move. Three honest tests:

**Do you have the cash flow?** Deferring an extra $3,250 means $3,250 less take-home. If funding it means carrying a credit card balance, the math fails immediately.

**Where will that money be taxed on the way out?** This is the real question, and it is the one most calculators skip. Ages 60 to 63 are frequently peak earning years — and they are also the last clean years before Medicare at 65, before Social Security claiming decisions, and before RMDs at 73. Pre-tax deferrals now reduce today's tax bill but enlarge the traditional balance you will eventually be forced to draw down. If you are already staring at a large traditional balance and a sizable RMD, adding more pre-tax dollars in your early 60s can mean converting a 22% problem today into a 24% problem at 75.

For some people the answer is to take the full pre-tax deferral. For others it is to take the deferral as Roth voluntarily, even if the wage threshold does not force them to. For a third group, the better use of that cash flow is a Roth conversion in a low-income year instead.

**What does it do to the rest of the plan?** Deferrals lower your MAGI, which ripples into ACA premium tax credits if you are not yet on Medicare, and into the IRMAA determination two years ahead of your Medicare start. Those effects can be worth more than the deduction itself — or they can be irrelevant. It depends on where your income actually lands.

## The part worth doing this month

You do not need a model to do the first three steps, and they expire on a calendar:

1. Figure out which calendar years are your window. The year you turn 60 through the year you turn 63.
2. Confirm your plan offers the provision. Ask HR directly.
3. Check your 2026 FICA wages from that employer against $155,000 to know whether your 2027 catch-up must be Roth.

Then set the election before the first January payroll run, because a catch-up window you discover in November is a window you have mostly already spent.

The fourth step — whether the money should go in pre-tax or Roth, and what it does to your tax bill at 75 — is the one that genuinely requires projecting forward. Four years. Then the door closes on its own.
