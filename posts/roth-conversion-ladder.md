Most people assume their tax rate drops in retirement. For many, it actually goes up — and the culprit is a pile of tax-deferred money they spent decades building.

If your retirement savings sit mostly in traditional 401(k)s and IRAs, every dollar you withdraw gets taxed as ordinary income. Add Social Security (up to 85% of which is taxable), required minimum distributions that force withdrawals whether you need the money or not, and Medicare surcharges triggered by income thresholds — and you may find yourself in a higher bracket than your working years.

A Roth conversion ladder is one of the most effective tools for taking control of this problem. Here's how it works, when it makes sense, and how to figure out the right amounts for your specific situation.

## What a Roth Conversion Ladder Actually Is

The concept is simple: you move money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth IRA, pay taxes on the converted amount now, and then enjoy tax-free withdrawals later.

The "ladder" part comes from doing this strategically over multiple years — converting just enough each year to fill up lower tax brackets without pushing yourself into expensive ones.

For example, if you're married filing jointly and your taxable income (after deductions) would otherwise be $50,000 in a given year, you have room to convert roughly $40,000 more before you leave the 12% federal bracket and enter the 22% bracket (based on 2026 brackets). That's $40,000 moved from forever-taxed to never-taxed-again, at a relatively low rate.

Do this for five, eight, or twelve years and you can shift a substantial portion of your retirement savings into Roth accounts — dramatically reducing your lifetime tax burden.

## The Window Most People Miss

The Roth conversion ladder works best during a specific window: the years between when you stop earning a full salary and when Social Security and RMDs kick in.

For early retirees in the FIRE community, this window can be enormous — potentially 15 to 20 years between leaving work in their 40s and reaching age 73 when RMDs begin. Even for traditional retirees who stop working at 62 or 65, there's often a gap of several years where income is unusually low.

During this window, your tax brackets are at their emptiest. Every dollar you convert fills those low brackets before they get crowded by Social Security and mandatory withdrawals.

Miss the window, and you may find yourself converting at 22% or 24% — or worse, not converting at all and watching RMDs push you into brackets you never expected.

## The Five Variables That Determine Your Optimal Conversion Amount

Figuring out how much to convert each year isn't a simple formula. It depends on the interaction of several moving parts:

### 1. Current-Year Income

Any earned income, pension payments, rental income, or investment income you already have fills your brackets first. Conversions stack on top. You need a clear picture of your baseline taxable income before deciding how much room you have.

### 2. Future RMD Projections

RMDs are calculated based on your account balance at year-end divided by a life expectancy factor. If you don't convert, your traditional IRA balance keeps growing — and so do your mandatory withdrawals. Modeling this forward 10 to 20 years shows you exactly how large those forced distributions will be.

### 3. Social Security Timing

When you claim Social Security affects both your income and your conversion window. Delaying to age 70 maximizes your benefit but also creates more taxable income later. The interplay between conversion strategy and claiming strategy is one of the most consequential financial decisions retirees face.

### 4. State Income Taxes

This is the variable most people overlook. Nine states have no income tax at all. Others have flat rates. Many have progressive brackets that don't align with federal ones. If you live in California or New York, conversions cost significantly more than in Florida or Texas. Some retirees even time relocations to coincide with their heaviest conversion years.

### 5. Medicare IRMAA Thresholds

Medicare premiums jump at specific income thresholds (called IRMAA brackets). A conversion that pushes you $1 over the line can cost an extra $2,000 to $4,000 per year in premiums — for both you and your spouse. Smart conversion planning stays just under these cliffs.

## Why Spreadsheets Break Down Here

Many financially savvy people try to model Roth conversions in a spreadsheet. It works for a year or two, but the interactions between federal brackets, state taxes, Social Security taxation, IRMAA thresholds, and RMD growth create a combinatorial problem that's genuinely hard to track manually.

Change one variable — say, delaying Social Security by two years — and every year of the conversion plan shifts. Add a spouse with their own accounts and timeline, and the complexity doubles.

This is where purpose-built forecasting tools earn their keep. You need something that models all 50 states' tax rules, handles progressive federal brackets year by year, projects RMD growth, and lets you compare scenarios side by side.

## A Practical Example

Meet Sarah, age 58, planning to retire at 60. She has $1.2 million in a traditional 401(k), $200,000 in a Roth IRA, and $150,000 in a taxable brokerage account. She and her husband plan to claim Social Security at 67.

Without conversions, her RMDs starting at 73 would be roughly $85,000 per year (assuming 5% growth), which combined with Social Security puts them solidly in the 22% federal bracket — possibly touching 24%.

With a conversion ladder from age 60 to 72, converting roughly $60,000 per year, she moves $780,000 to Roth over 13 years. Her RMDs shrink dramatically because the traditional balance is much lower. Most conversion dollars are taxed at 12%, and her post-72 income stays in the 12% bracket instead of 22%.

The tax savings over a 30-year retirement? Potentially over $150,000 — and that's before accounting for the tax-free growth inside the Roth.
