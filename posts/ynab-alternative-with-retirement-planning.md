YNAB does one thing with real conviction: it makes you decide what this month's dollars are for. For a lot of people, that method is the thing that finally made budgeting stick.

Then, usually a few years in, a different question shows up. Not "can we afford dinner out this month" but "when can I stop working?" And that question hits a wall, because YNAB was never built to answer it.

If that's the wall you've hit, this post is for you. Here is what YNAB does well, where it stops, and what to look for in an alternative that handles both halves of your financial life.

## What YNAB does well

Credit where it's due. YNAB's envelope method — give every dollar a job, age your money, roll with the punches — is a genuinely effective way to run a month. The community is loyal for a reason, and if zero-based budgeting is the discipline that keeps your spending honest, that's worth a lot.

Nothing below is an argument that YNAB is bad at budgeting. It's an argument that budgeting is only half the job.

## The wall: your investments are just a balance

Open YNAB and look at your 401(k), IRA, or brokerage account. What you'll find is a single number. YNAB's own documentation describes investment accounts as "tracking accounts" — the balance counts toward your net worth, and that is the extent of it.

No holdings. No returns. No sense of whether your portfolio did its job last year. And nothing that connects those balances to a date — no model of what happens when you stop contributing and start withdrawing, what taxes will take, or how long the money lasts.

That's a reasonable design choice for a budgeting app. But it means the most important numbers in your financial life sit in YNAB as dead weight. The app can tell you everything about last month and nothing about the next thirty years.

The gap widens as you get closer to the finish line. In your thirties, a balance is enough. At 55, the questions are specific: which account do I draw from first, what do Roth conversions save me, what does claiming Social Security at 67 versus 70 do to the plan, what happens to my taxes once required minimum distributions kick in. A tracking account has no opinion on any of it.

## The two-app workaround, and what it costs

The standard fix is to run two tools: YNAB for the daily budget, plus a separate retirement planner for the long-term picture.

It works, but you pay for it three ways.

You pay twice, literally. YNAB runs $14.99 a month or $109 a year as of November 2026, and a serious planning tool is a second subscription on top.

You maintain two copies of the truth. The planner needs your annual spending as an input, and you type it in by hand — an estimate of the very number your budgeting app tracks to the penny. When your spending changes, the plan doesn't know until you remember to update it.

And the two never check each other. Your budget says groceries ran hot this year; your retirement plan still assumes the old number. Small drift, compounded over decades, is how a plan ends up confidently wrong.

The spending data your budget already captures is the single best input a retirement forecast can have. Splitting them across two apps throws that advantage away.

## What to look for in an alternative that does both

If you're shopping for one app to replace the pair, hold it to this standard:

**Real account sync, including brokerage.** Bank and card connections are table stakes. The differentiator is investment accounts synced at the holdings level — positions and transactions, not a hand-updated balance. (Tally connects banks through Plaid's 2,000+ institutions and syncs brokerage accounts through SnapTrade; it also imports from Tiller, Google Sheets, and CSV if your history lives in a spreadsheet.)

**Categorization you can trust, with a review step.** If you're coming from YNAB, you're used to approving transactions. Look for AI categorization that drafts the work but still lets you review and correct it — automation without the review step is how categories quietly rot.

**A budget with a yearly spine.** Months are not identical: insurance bills in June, gifts in December. An annual budget broken into unequal months, with budget-versus-actuals tracking, mirrors how spending actually behaves.

**A lifetime forecast that takes taxes seriously.** This is the half YNAB doesn't have, so don't accept a toy version of it. A compound-interest widget is not a plan. Look for a forecast that models federal and state taxes, required minimum distributions at the SECURE 2.0 ages, Social Security timing, and Roth conversion trade-offs — and that shows you a runway: the date, under stated assumptions, your money runs out.

**Stress testing, not just one projection.** A single average-case line hides the risk. What-if scenarios and Monte Carlo simulation show you the range of outcomes, which is what you actually plan against.

**Room for two.** If you share your finances, the forecast should handle a household: separate life expectancies, per-partner accounts, and what the plan looks like for whoever outlives whom.

**An import path.** Years of categorized history is the real switching cost. An alternative should read your YNAB export so you keep the record you built.

But take the checklist to any tool you're considering. Most budgeting apps fail it on the forecast half; most planning tools fail it on the budget half.

## When you should stay with YNAB

An honest comparison includes this part.

If envelope discipline is the point for you — if assigning every incoming dollar to a job is the ritual that keeps your household on track — YNAB is still the sharpest tool for that specific method, and Tally doesn't imitate it. Tally budgets by year and by month against your actuals; it doesn't ask you to assign each paycheck as it lands.

If your finances are simple, retirement is decades out, and the long-term question doesn't itch yet, a budgeting-only app may be all you need for now.

The time to switch is when you keep asking a question your current app can't answer. For most people that question is some version of: are we going to be okay?
