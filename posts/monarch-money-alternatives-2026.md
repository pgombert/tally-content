# Monarch Money Alternatives in 2026: What to Check Before You Switch

If you're searching for a Monarch Money alternative, you're probably not angry at Monarch. It's a well-built app. The connections are reliable, the interface is clean, and sharing a budget with a partner works the way it should.

What usually sends people looking is one of two things. The price: Monarch's Core plan runs $99.99 a year, and the features many people actually came for — retirement forecasting, life-event modeling — sit in a Plus tier at $199.99 a year. Or the ceiling: you set up your budget, you categorized a year of transactions, and then you asked the question every budget eventually leads to — *am I going to be okay?* — and found the app wasn't built to answer it on the plan you're paying for.

Before you export your data and start over somewhere else, it's worth getting clear on what you're actually shopping for. Switching apps costs you an afternoon and a year of muscle memory. Switching to the wrong app costs you that twice.

## First, decide which problem you're solving

Money apps cluster into three groups, and most frustration comes from expecting one group to do another group's job.

**Budgeting apps** track accounts, categorize spending, and tell you where this month went. Monarch's Core plan lives here, and so does YNAB, which goes deep on envelope-style budgeting and stays there on purpose.

**Retirement planners** project decades ahead — when you can stop working, how long the money lasts, what taxes will take. Tools like Boldin and ProjectionLab live here. They're built for the forecast, not for Tuesday's grocery run, and most don't want your daily transactions at all.

**Both-in-one apps** connect the two: your real spending feeds your long-term plan. This is the smallest group, because it's the hardest to build. Tally — our app — lives here, which is the honest disclosure to make before you read another word of a comparison written by a founder. I'll keep the claims specific and checkable.

If all you want is cleaner budgeting at a lower price, your shortlist is one set of apps. If you've started asking the retirement question, it's a different set — and the rest of this post is about how to compare that set properly.

## What to check in any alternative

### 1. Where the forecast gets its numbers

A retirement projection is only as good as the spending number you feed it, and most people guess that number badly. An app that already watches your actual transactions doesn't have to guess — your real spending rate becomes the baseline of your plan, and when your spending changes, the forecast moves with it.

If the planner you're considering asks you to type in "annual expenses" as a single number you made up, that's not a dealbreaker — but know that your plan is resting on it.

### 2. How it handles taxes

This is where forecasting tools separate, and it's worth reading the fine print. Monarch's forecasting documentation describes flat tax-rate assumptions you choose yourself — by default, 15% on capital gains and 20% on withdrawals from tax-deferred accounts.

Flat rates are a reasonable simplification, but retirement is exactly where flat rates mislead. Real withdrawals fill real brackets. A $60,000 withdrawal year and a $160,000 withdrawal year face very different effective rates. Required minimum distributions can push you into brackets you didn't plan for. State taxes vary from 0% to north of 10%. Whether a Roth conversion makes sense depends on the gap between your bracket today and your bracket at RMD age — a question a flat rate cannot answer by construction.

Tally computes federal brackets, all 50 states, FICA, the 3.8% net investment income tax, RMDs at the SECURE 2.0 ages, and Social Security taxation inside every forecast, and runs Roth conversion analysis against your actual numbers. If taxes are a small part of your retirement picture, flat rates may be fine. If you have most of your savings in tax-deferred accounts, they are not a small part.

### 3. Whether "what if" means one path or thousands

A single projection shows one future: markets return X%, you spend Y, done. Useful, but fragile — retirement outcomes hinge on sequence risk, which only shows up when you run many futures. Look for Monte Carlo simulation, not just a line that goes up. And look for what-if scenarios you can stack: retire two years earlier, sell the house at 70, one partner stops working at 58.

### 4. Couples, if you are one

If you're planning with a partner, check whether the app models you as two people or one blob. Different ages, different life expectancies, different Social Security claims — these change the plan materially. Tally models each partner separately, down to a per-partner runway.

### 5. The import path

Whatever you choose, you shouldn't lose your history. Monarch exports your transactions as CSV. Tally imports Monarch's export directly, along with YNAB's, plus Tiller and Google Sheets for the spreadsheet crowd. Account connections run through Plaid (2,000+ institutions) with SnapTrade for brokerage sync. Expect any serious alternative to offer something similar; a clean import turns the switching afternoon into twenty minutes.

### 6. The all-in price

Compare what you'd actually pay for the features you actually want. The common pattern is a budgeting app plus a separate planner, each with its own subscription. Monarch solves it with tiers: $99.99 for budgeting, $199.99 with forecasting. Tally is one plan: $7.99 a month, or $79 a year with two months free, forecasting included. Price the problem, not the app.

## How to switch without losing anything

A few practical notes from watching people migrate, in any direction.

Export everything before you cancel. Monarch's CSV export covers transactions; download it while your subscription is live, because reading your own history shouldn't be a race against a billing date. Grab your account balances and category list too — a screenshot is fine.

Expect to spend your first hour on categories, not accounts. Connecting institutions is fast. The real work is mapping your old categories to the new app's, and it's worth doing deliberately: your category history is what makes a spending-based forecast accurate from day one. Tally's AI categorization takes a first pass and routes anything uncertain into a review queue rather than guessing silently — whatever app you pick, make sure you can see and correct what the machine decided.

Run both apps through one statement cycle. Two weeks of overlap costs a few dollars and catches the broken connection or the miscategorized paycheck while your old app is still there to check against.

And give the forecast a real audit before you trust it. Put the same inputs into the new tool and your old one, and make sure you understand why the answers differ. Usually the difference is taxes. Now you know which tool is doing the harder math.
