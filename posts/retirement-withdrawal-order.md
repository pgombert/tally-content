Ask ten people which account to spend first in retirement and you'll hear the same answer ten times: taxable brokerage first, traditional IRA second, Roth last.

It's tidy. It's everywhere. And for a lot of retirees, it quietly costs tens of thousands of dollars in extra tax.

Here's why the standard order exists, where it breaks, and the question that should replace it.

## Where the standard order comes from

The logic is simple: tax-advantaged accounts grow without annual tax drag, so you want money sheltered as long as possible. Spend the brokerage account, where dividends and gains get taxed every year anyway. Let the IRA compound. Save the Roth — the best tax treatment of all — for the very end.

Each piece of that logic is true. The problem is what happens to your tax return while you follow it.

## The wasted-deduction years

Say you retire at 65 and live off your brokerage account for the first eight years. Your income on paper is tiny: some dividends, some interest, the taxable slice of whatever shares you sell. Many retirees in this phase owe little or no federal income tax.

That sounds like winning. It isn't.

In 2026, a married couple filing jointly gets a $32,200 standard deduction. If both spouses are 65 or older, the new senior deduction adds $6,000 each through 2028 (it phases out above $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income for joint filers). That's $44,200 of income the federal government would have taxed at 0% — this year, use it or lose it.

A couple spending down only their brokerage account uses almost none of it. Year after year, a five-figure tax-free allowance expires unused. There's no carryforward. It's just gone.

## The RMD pileup

Meanwhile, the traditional IRA you're "preserving" keeps compounding — and every dollar of growth is a dollar of future ordinary income.

Required minimum distributions now start at 73 for people born 1951 through 1958, and at 75 for those born in 1960 or later. (Congress left 1959 ambiguous; proposed IRS rules put that year at 73.) When they start, they're not optional — the excise tax on a missed RMD is 25% of the shortfall, or 10% if you correct it quickly.

Run the arithmetic on a $900,000 IRA left untouched from 65 to 73. At 6% growth it's roughly $1.4 million when RMDs begin, forcing a first-year withdrawal in the neighborhood of $54,000 — stacked on top of Social Security, of which up to 85% can be taxable. The couple who paid almost nothing in tax for eight years can land in the 22% bracket or higher for the rest of their lives, with Medicare premium surcharges often riding along.

The standard order didn't avoid tax. It deferred it, concentrated it, and handed it back at a higher rate.

## A better question: what fills your brackets this year?

Instead of "which account do I drain first," ask: "how much income can I recognize this year at rates I'll never see again?"

In 2026, for a married couple filing jointly, the key lines are:

- **$44,200 at 0%** — the standard deduction plus two senior deductions (if both are 65+) wipe out that much ordinary income entirely. IRA withdrawals up to that line can be federal-tax-free.
- **12% up to $100,800 of taxable income** — ordinary income beyond the deductions is cheap by historical standards.
- **0% on long-term capital gains up to $98,900 of taxable income** — brokerage gains below that line cost nothing federally. (For single filers: $16,100 standard deduction, $6,000 senior deduction, 12% bracket to $50,400, 0% gains to $49,450.)

The efficient move in the early years is usually a blend: pull enough from the traditional IRA (or convert it to Roth) to use up the 0% and low brackets, then cover remaining spending from the brokerage account, where long-term gains may also be taxed at 0%.

Take that same couple, both 66, spending $80,000 a year. A blended order might withdraw about $44,000 from the IRA — federal tax: roughly zero — and raise the rest by selling appreciated shares, with gains taxed at 0% as long as taxable income stays under $98,900. They spend the same $80,000 and shrink the IRA that was on course to generate the RMD pileup. The brackets get used instead of wasted.

This is the same logic behind Roth conversions in the gap years — we've written about [the conversion sweet spot](/blog/roth-conversion-sweet-spot-2026) — but it applies even if you never convert a dollar. The withdrawal order itself is a tax decision.

## The gap years are the whole game

The window between your last paycheck and the start of Social Security and RMDs is when you have the most control over your taxable income that you will ever have. Retire at 62 with RMDs at 75 and you have thirteen years of brackets to fill deliberately.

Every year in that window is a one-shot decision. Which account you tap, how much you convert, when you claim Social Security, which gains you harvest — each choice reshapes every year after it. A rule of thumb that ignores the calendar can't see any of that.

## Where the Roth actually fits

None of this demotes the Roth. "Roth last" is the one part of the standard order that usually survives contact with the math, because tax-free growth is worth the most over the longest horizon and Roth accounts have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime.

But the Roth earns its keep earlier as a release valve. In a year when one more dollar of ordinary income would trigger something expensive — pushing more Social Security into taxation, crossing a Medicare surcharge line, spilling gains out of the 0% zone — covering the shortfall from the Roth keeps your taxable income exactly where you want it. A Roth you never touch is a parking spot; a Roth you deploy at the right moments is a steering wheel.

State tax adds one more layer: a withdrawal that's free federally may still be taxed by your state, and some states exempt retirement income that others don't. The order that wins in Texas can lose in California.

## Couples: the order matters twice

There's a second clock running for married couples. When one spouse dies, the survivor files single: roughly half the standard deduction, brackets that compress at half the income, often most of the same portfolio. An IRA balance that was comfortable inside married-filing-jointly brackets can be punishing inside single ones.

That argues for drawing down traditional balances faster while both spouses are alive and the wide brackets are available — the opposite of what "save the IRA for later" does. Any honest plan has to model both lifetimes separately, not assume one household forever.

## Rules of thumb versus your actual numbers

The right withdrawal order depends on your account mix, your ages, your state, your Social Security timing, your spending, and the brackets in force each year. That's not a flowchart problem. It's a modeling problem — year by year, until the money runs out or you do.
