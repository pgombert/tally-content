If you spent a career at one company and your 401(k) is stuffed with that company's stock, you have a tax decision most people never learn about — and it has an expiration date.

The default move is to roll the whole 401(k) into an IRA. Clean, simple, one statement. But if a big chunk of that balance is employer stock you bought cheap decades ago, rolling it into an IRA converts every dollar of that appreciation into **ordinary income**, taxable at your regular rate whenever you withdraw it. There's an alternative — the net unrealized appreciation (NUA) election — that can move most of that gain into the long-term capital gains system instead.

It's not always the better choice. But it's a one-shot decision, and getting it wrong is permanent.

## What NUA actually is

NUA is the difference between what your 401(k) paid for the employer stock (the cost basis) and what it's worth when you take it out.

Say your plan bought shares over 25 years for a total of $90,000. Today they're worth $600,000. Your NUA is $510,000.

Under the NUA rules, you can distribute those shares **in kind** — the actual shares, moved to a regular taxable brokerage account, not sold and not rolled over. When you do:

- The **cost basis** ($90,000) is taxable right now as ordinary income.
- The **NUA** ($510,000) is not taxed at distribution. It's taxed later, when you sell, at **long-term capital gains rates** — and it gets long-term treatment automatically, no matter how long you hold the shares after the distribution.

Compare that to the IRA rollover path: all $600,000 eventually comes out as ordinary income, taxed at your marginal rate, and starting at 73 required minimum distributions force it out whether you want it or not.

## The math, at 2026 rates

Here's why the spread matters. For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, and ordinary income still runs through seven rates. For married couples filing jointly, the 22% bracket covers $100,801 to $211,400, the 24% bracket runs to $403,550, and the 32% bracket runs to $512,450.

Long-term capital gains sit on a separate, much friendlier schedule. For 2026, the 0% ceiling is $49,450 of taxable income for single filers and $98,900 for married filing jointly. The 15% rate runs up to $545,500 and $613,700 respectively, with 20% above that.

So a retired couple with moderate other income might pay 24% on ordinary dollars and 15% on capital gain dollars. On $510,000 of appreciation, that nine-point spread is roughly $46,000 — and that's before considering that capital gains can be spread across years, offset with losses, or partially harvested inside the 0% band.

One caveat that trips people up: NUA gain is investment income, so it counts toward the 3.8% net investment income tax, which applies once modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married couples filing jointly. Those thresholds are not indexed to inflation. A large NUA sale in one year can push you over.

## The three conditions, and why most people blow them

NUA is governed by a narrow set of requirements under Internal Revenue Code section 402(e)(4). Miss one and the election is gone.

**1. A triggering event.** You need separation from service, reaching age 59½, disability, or death. No triggering event, no NUA.

**2. A lump-sum distribution.** The entire balance of the plan — all of it, including every similar plan with that employer — must be distributed within a single calendar year. The employer stock goes in kind to a taxable brokerage account; the rest can roll to an IRA. But it all has to clear inside one tax year.

This is where strategies die. If the in-kind share transfer settles in late December and the residual cash rolls over the first week of January, you have two tax years, not one, and the election is lost on the whole balance.

**3. No partial distributions after the trigger.** If you took a distribution after the triggering event in a prior year, you generally have to wait for the next triggering event. Retire at 60, pull $20,000 out "just to cover a gap," and you may have closed the window until another qualifying event occurs.

There's a fourth practical wrinkle: the cost basis portion is taxable ordinary income in the distribution year, and plans apply mandatory withholding to the taxable part of a distribution. Have outside cash ready — paying that bill by selling the shares you just distributed defeats part of the point.

## When NUA wins, and when it doesn't

NUA is strongest when:

- **The basis is low relative to value.** The common planning rule of thumb is that NUA favors a cost basis under roughly 25–30% of current share value. The lower the basis, the smaller the immediate ordinary-income hit and the bigger the gain that escapes into capital gains treatment.
- **You're older and the time horizon to withdrawal is short.** The IRA rollover's advantage is decades of tax-deferred compounding. If you're 62 and will start drawing within a few years, there isn't enough runway for deferral to beat the rate arbitrage.
- **You expect high ordinary income in retirement.** A pension, Social Security, and large traditional balances pushing you into 24% or 32% all widen the gap between your ordinary rate and 15%.
- **You want to defuse RMDs.** Shares in a taxable account aren't subject to required minimum distributions. Moving $600,000 out of the pre-tax system permanently lowers every future RMD.

NUA is weaker when the basis is high, when you're young enough that another 25 years of deferral matters more than rate treatment, or when the ordinary income from the basis would land in an unusually bad year.

## The thing nobody tells you about death

Here's the asymmetry that surprises people. Appreciated stock in a regular taxable account typically gets a basis step-up at the owner's death. **NUA does not.** The net unrealized appreciation retains its character as income in respect of a decedent — heirs inherit the embedded gain and owe capital gains tax on it when they sell. Only the appreciation that happens after the distribution gets the step-up.

So NUA isn't a wealth-transfer strategy. It's a strategy for someone who intends to actually sell the shares and spend the money, or to diversify out of a concentrated position over time.

## Sequencing it with everything else

The NUA year is a loud year. You're adding the full cost basis to your ordinary income, and if you sell shares that same year, capital gains on top. That has knock-on effects across the plan:

- **Medicare premiums.** Income in the NUA year flows into the IRMAA calculation two years later. If you're 63 or older, a big NUA distribution shows up as higher Part B and Part D premiums at 65 or 66.
- **ACA subsidies.** If you're retiring before Medicare and buying marketplace coverage, the income spike can wipe out a year of premium tax credits.
- **Roth conversions.** The NUA year is usually a poor year to also convert. The conversion and the basis recognition stack on top of each other.

The sensible pattern for a lot of people is to take the NUA distribution in one deliberately noisy year, accept the hit, then use the following low-income years for conversions and for selling shares inside the 0% and 15% capital gains bands.

There's also a reason to separate the tax question from the investment question entirely. If 40% of your net worth sits in one employer's stock, concentration risk is probably a bigger threat to your retirement than a nine-point rate difference. NUA gives you a tax-efficient way to unwind that position — but unwinding it is the point.

## How to decide

Three numbers tell you most of what you need:

1. Your plan's **cost basis** in the employer stock. Request it in writing. Plans track it, but you usually have to ask.
2. Your projected **ordinary marginal rate** in retirement, including Social Security taxation and future RMDs.
3. Your projected **capital gains rate** in the years you'd actually sell.

Then run both paths across the rest of your life — not just the first year. The IRA rollover looks better on a one-year view almost every time, because NUA front-loads a tax bill. The comparison only means something over the full withdrawal horizon, with RMDs, Social Security, IRMAA, and state taxes in the model.
