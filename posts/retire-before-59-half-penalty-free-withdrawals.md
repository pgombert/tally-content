Ask someone why they can't retire at 52 and you'll usually hear the same answer: "My money is locked up until 59½."

It isn't. The 10% early-withdrawal penalty is real — take money out of a traditional IRA or 401(k) before age 59½ and the IRS normally adds 10% on top of the ordinary income tax you'd owe anyway. But the tax code has always had doors built into that wall. Four of them are wide enough to fund an early retirement. None of them are loopholes. They're printed in the statute, and the IRS publishes the rules for each.

Here's how each one works, who it fits, and where people get hurt.

## Door 1: The rule of 55

If you leave your job during or after the calendar year you turn 55 — quit, laid off, fired, doesn't matter — you can take money from **that employer's** 401(k) or 403(b) with no 10% penalty. You'll still owe ordinary income tax. You just skip the penalty.

For qualified public safety employees — police, firefighters, EMS, certain federal officers — the age drops to 50. Since SECURE 2.0, those workers can also qualify with 25 years of service under the plan, even before 50.

The fine print matters here:

The rule applies only to the plan of the job you just left. Your IRA doesn't get this exception, and neither does the 401(k) from a job you left at 52. Which leads to the classic mistake: rolling that final 401(k) into an IRA "to simplify things" — and locking the door behind you. Money that was penalty-free at 55 becomes penalty-locked until 59½ the moment it lands in the IRA.

The other catch is the plan itself. The IRS permits these withdrawals; your plan doesn't have to. Some plans only allow a single lump-sum distribution after you leave. Before you build a retirement around the rule of 55, read your plan's distribution options or call the administrator and ask one question: "Do you allow partial withdrawals after separation?"

Best fit: people retiring at 55–59 with a healthy balance in their final employer's plan.

## Door 2: 72(t) — substantially equal periodic payments

Section 72(t) of the tax code lets you start penalty-free withdrawals from an IRA at any age, provided you commit to a series of "substantially equal periodic payments" — a fixed annual draw, calculated one of three IRS-approved ways:

- **RMD method** — balance divided by your life-expectancy factor, recalculated yearly. Smallest payment.
- **Fixed amortization** — your balance amortized over your life expectancy at a chosen interest rate. Largest payment.
- **Fixed annuitization** — balance divided by an annuity factor. In between.

Since IRS Notice 2022-6, the interest rate can be anything up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate. That 5% floor made 72(t) meaningfully more useful than it was a decade ago.

Concrete numbers: a 50-year-old with a $500,000 IRA, using the amortization method at 5% and the IRS single life table (36.2 years), can draw about $30,100 a year, penalty-free. The RMD method on the same account pays about $13,800 in year one. Those are illustrations, not quotes — run your own numbers before filing anything.

Now the teeth. Once you start, you're locked in for the **longer** of five years or until you reach 59½. Start at 50 and you're committed for nine and a half years. Break the schedule — take an extra dollar, skip a year, roll new money into the account — and the IRS retroactively applies the 10% penalty to *every* payment you've taken, plus interest. There's one escape hatch: a one-time switch to the smaller RMD method if the fixed payment is draining the account too fast.

One structural tip: 72(t) applies per account. Split your IRA in two first, and you can size the payment from one account while the other stays flexible for emergencies.

Best fit: early retirees in their 40s or early 50s with large pre-tax balances and stable spending. It rewards people who plan and punishes people who improvise.

## Door 3: The Roth conversion ladder

This is the FIRE community's favorite, and it needs the most lead time.

The pieces: Roth IRA **contributions** can come out any time, tax- and penalty-free. **Converted** amounts — money moved from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth — can come out penalty-free once that conversion is five tax years old (or once you hit 59½, whichever comes first). Each conversion starts its own clock, which begins on January 1 of the year you convert. **Earnings** stay put until 59½.

So the ladder: each year of early retirement, convert one year of future spending from your traditional IRA to your Roth. You'll pay ordinary income tax on the conversion — ideally at low rates, because you're retired and your taxable income is low. Five tax years later, that rung unlocks. Convert every year and by year five you have a conveyor belt of penalty-free money arriving annually.

The obvious question: what do you live on during the first five years? That's the bridge problem, and it's usually solved with a taxable brokerage account, Roth contributions you made during your working years, or one of the other doors above.

Two things people miss. First, conversions are taxable income, so a big conversion year can spill into higher brackets and — if you're buying health insurance on an ACA exchange — shrink your premium subsidy. The conversion size is a tax decision, not just a cash-flow decision. Second, because the clock starts January 1 of the conversion year, a conversion made in December is already almost a year old. Timing matters.

Best fit: people retiring very early — 40s or younger — with time to build the bridge, and low-income years to convert cheaply in.

## Door 4: The one almost nobody mentions — the governmental 457(b)

If you work for a state or local government and have a 457(b) plan, you may not need any of the strategies above. Distributions from a governmental 457(b) after you separate from service are simply **not subject** to the 10% additional tax, at any age. (The exception: money you rolled into the 457(b) from a 401(k) or IRA keeps its penalty rules.) You owe ordinary income tax and nothing else.

If you have one of these, it's your first-out money. Spend it before you touch anything with a penalty attached.

## Choosing your door

The right answer is usually a sequence, not a single strategy: taxable brokerage and Roth contributions first, a 457(b) if you have one, the rule of 55 if your timing fits, a conversion ladder built during the bridge years, and 72(t) as the precision tool when the others don't cover the gap.

And every door has the same hidden cost: each one turns pre-tax money into taxable income, which interacts with your tax bracket, your ACA subsidy, and the Roth conversions you might rather do instead. The penalty is the easy part to avoid. The sequencing is where the real money is won or lost.
